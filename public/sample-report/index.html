01 / Executive summary

Protect payments. Prove recovery. Close unnecessary access.

The example business can continue trading, but three avoidable weaknesses deserve early management attention: supplier payment changes depend on email, recovery has not been demonstrated, and access is not consistently removed when people leave. These are linked business risks, not simply a shopping list of security tools.

Illustrative management view

Act on the highest-impact gaps first.

Prioritise finance safeguards and a safe recovery exercise. Run account clean-up alongside them. Address device coverage, external sharing and incident readiness through the same 90-day plan.

No overall “cyber health” percentage is assigned: mixed evidence and different business impacts would make a single number misleading.

What is already helping

The scenario includes an external IT provider, MFA enforcement on 42 of 48 staff accounts, endpoint protection reporting on 39 of 44 laptops, and configured backup jobs for two of three critical services. These are useful starting points, not proof that every control works.

What management needs to approve

  • A finance-controlled callback and second-approval process for supplier bank changes, starting immediately.
  • Named service owners and a provider-assisted recovery exercise with an agreed safe test environment.
  • Time for the office manager and IT provider to reconcile accounts and devices, with exceptions recorded rather than silently accepted.

The ratings below describe this invented scenario only. They are not forecasts of an incident, a validated maturity score or a comparison with other businesses.

02 / Business & scope

A small team. Several critical dependencies.

Cedar & Coast Wholesale is an illustrative South African distributor with 48 staff across a Cape Town office and warehouse. It processes trade orders, dispatches stock and pays suppliers. An outsourced IT provider supports the environment; internal managers remain accountable for business decisions.

Example environment

  • 48 named staff cloud accounts and four additional enabled accounts belonging to former staff.
  • Four separate privileged administrator accounts and three non-interactive application identities: 59 directory identities in total.
  • 44 managed-laptop inventory entries and two network storage appliances. Warehouse staff share shifts, not user credentials.
  • Three critical services: cloud email, cloud accounting and a shared order/document store.

Business dependencies

  • Finance: email supports invoice handling and supplier bank-change requests; accounting holds the supplier master.
  • Operations: the order/document store holds dispatch schedules and customer order records.
  • Management: the provider holds technical knowledge, but no tested business-led incident runbook exists in this scenario.

Discovery approach represented

  1. Understand: example interviews with the managing director, finance lead, operations lead, office manager and IT provider.
  2. Review: illustrative exports, policy settings, inventory records, backup summaries and a small payment-change sample.
  3. Challenge: compare claimed controls with the example evidence, identify gaps and note what remains unknown.
  4. Prioritise: discuss plausible business consequences, assign a scenario rating and agree owners and closure evidence.

In scope: identity and access, supplier-payment workflow, laptop control coverage, recovery arrangements, external file sharing and incident responsibilities for the environment above.

Out of scope: penetration testing, vulnerability exploitation, phishing simulations, source-code review, physical security, full network configuration review and legal compliance assessment. No live restore or adversarial test is represented. A real engagement would require agreed scope, authorisation and evidence-handling arrangements.

04 / How priorities work

A transparent discussion aid.

Illustrative current risk = likelihood × impact, each rated 1–5. Ratings consider the example gaps and existing controls over a notional next-12-month planning horizon. They are qualitative judgements, not calculated probabilities or loss estimates. This is not an industry benchmark.

Likelihood

  1. Rare: exceptional conditions and strong demonstrated safeguards.
  2. Unlikely: a plausible route, with substantial barriers.
  3. Possible: a credible route with partial safeguards.
  4. Likely: recurring exposure and a material control gap.
  5. Very likely: repeated relevant events or largely unimpeded exposure.

Business impact

  1. Minor: local inconvenience, normal work continues.
  2. Limited: one team needs a short-lived workaround.
  3. Material: a key process is disrupted or sensitive records are exposed.
  4. Major: several core activities stall, or a significant payment/data incident needs leadership intervention.
  5. Severe: sustained inability to trade or a business-threatening consequence.

Example bands: 1–6 Low · 7–12 Medium · 13–19 High · 20–25 Critical. These locally defined bands structure this sample only. “Low” still needs an owner. A score is not a deadline: dependencies, ease of containment and management judgement shape the roadmap.

Confidence is separate from severity. Moderate confidence means the invented records are consistent but no operating-effectiveness test is represented. Limited confidence means an important fact rests on an interview or missing evidence. None of the findings is presented as high-confidence technical validation.

05 / Detailed illustrative findings

From a concern to an owned action.

Each finding connects an invented observation to a business consequence, a practical response and evidence needed to close it. Role titles are suggested example owners, not real people or commitments.

F01 / Finance & identityHigh · 16 / 25

Supplier payment changes can bypass independent verification.

Likelihood 4 · Impact 4 · Moderate confidence · Owner: Finance lead

Illustrative evidence
E05: four of five example supplier bank-change records lack a logged callback and second approval. In example record PAY-04, an email request and supplier-master edit are present, but no independent verification note. E01: two of the six finance staff accounts are among the six staff MFA-policy exclusions.
Business consequence & rating rationale
A deceptive request or compromised mailbox could redirect a legitimate payment. Routine email exposure and weak independent checks support likelihood 4; payment disruption and supplier disputes support impact 4. This does not show fraud occurred.
Recommended response
Immediately require callbacks using an independently held supplier number, not one supplied in the change request. Separate request, verification and approval roles. Within 14 days, the IT provider should remove finance MFA exclusions through a supported enrolment process, verify enforcement and review relevant sign-in exceptions.
Closure evidence
Finance-approved procedure; finance-account policy export with no unapproved exclusions; the next five bank changes showing callback and separate approval. If fewer changes occur by day 30, review all available records and a walkthrough, and keep effectiveness verification open.
Delivery & remaining exposure
Finance lead owns the process; IT provider supports identity settings. Plan 1–2 internal working days plus separately agreed provider effort. These checks reduce an opportunity; they do not eliminate impersonation or override by authorised staff.
F02 / Business continuityHigh · 16 / 25

Successful backup jobs do not yet demonstrate recoverable operations.

Likelihood 4 · Impact 4 · Limited confidence · Owner: Operations lead

Illustrative evidence
E04: accounting and order-store job summaries show recent successful runs, but no documented restore demonstration exists for any of the three critical services in the example quarter. Email relies on retention, with independent recovery capability unconfirmed. Example operations interview: “We have not walked through dispatch without the shared order files.”
Business consequence & rating rationale
An outage, deletion or account compromise could leave order processing and invoicing unavailable longer than management expects. Untested dependencies and no demonstrated fallback justify likelihood 4 for exceeding an assumed recovery tolerance following disruption; reliance on several core services supports impact 4. This is not a forecast that an outage itself is likely.
Recommended response
By day 14, agree service order, recovery-time objectives (RTO: target time to resume) and recovery-point objectives (RPO: acceptable data age). Proposed discussion targets are order store 8 hours / 4 hours, accounting 24 hours / 24 hours and email 8 hours / 4 hours. These are unvalidated business requests, not current capabilities or service promises. Ask the provider to assess feasibility and cost before approval.
Closure evidence
An authorised, isolated restore exercise for each service; source and restore timestamps; elapsed recovery time; a business-owner check of usable records; documented dependencies and a runbook. Record gaps against approved targets. A successful file restore alone does not prove full service recovery.
Delivery & remaining exposure
Operations coordinates finance and the provider. Reserve 2–3 internal working days across planning and exercises; provider effort and safe test capacity require scoping. Start the plan now, demonstrate recovery during days 31–60, and keep unresolved service gaps open. Avoid destructive testing of production.
F03 / Access lifecycleMedium · 12 / 25

Departed staff retain unnecessary access.

Likelihood 3 · Impact 4 · Moderate confidence · Owner: Office manager

Illustrative evidence
E02: all four known leavers in the example quarter remain enabled in the directory. Example identity LEAVER-03 has an active account and order-folder group membership after its recorded leaving date. No sign-in investigation is represented, so use or misuse is unknown.
Business consequence & rating rationale
Unneeded identities provide a route into customer and operational data. Access exists, but no misuse is shown: likelihood 3. Broad access to records and business processes supports impact 4.
Recommended response
Within seven days, reconcile the four accounts with HR and system owners, transfer business-owned records safely, disable unneeded access and revoke active sessions through the provider. Introduce a dated leaver checklist covering cloud services, shared access and devices, with a business-owner sign-off.
Closure evidence
Approved disposition for all four identities; provider records of access removal and session revocation; an account-to-staff reconciliation; a completed walkthrough of the next leaver process. Review application identities separately instead of treating them as staff accounts.
Delivery & remaining exposure
Office manager is accountable; IT provider performs technical changes. Allow one internal working day initially, then a short monthly review. Shared credentials and unmanaged application access may remain undiscovered outside this scope.
F04 / Device hygieneMedium · 12 / 25

Device blind spots prevent a dependable update baseline.

Likelihood 3 · Impact 4 · Moderate confidence · Owner: IT service owner

Illustrative evidence
E03: 39 of 44 laptops report to the protection console. Of those 39, 35 meet the example 30-day update target and four are overdue. The other five are unknown. Example asset LAP-041 appears in inventory but has no current console check-in; this is a visibility gap, not proof it is infected or unprotected.
Business consequence & rating rationale
Unseen or overdue devices may provide an entry point to shared services. Partial coverage supports likelihood 3; potential disruption across core workflows supports impact 4. No specific exploitable vulnerability is asserted.
Recommended response
By day 30, locate the five devices, confirm ownership and status, and repair management reporting or document retirement. Schedule updates for the four overdue devices with a rollback plan. Use urgency based on exposure and supplier advisories rather than treating the example 30-day target as universal guidance.
Closure evidence
A reconciled 44-entry inventory with each asset reporting or formally retired; dated update and protection exports; approved, time-limited exceptions with compensating controls. If devices are retired, revise the denominator and preserve the reason for the change.
Delivery & remaining exposure
The nominated internal IT service owner coordinates the external provider. Allow 1–2 internal working days plus scoped provider work and maintenance windows. Console coverage alone does not prove detection or response effectiveness.
F05 / Information handlingMedium · 9 / 25

Anonymous links outlive a clear business purpose.

Likelihood 3 · Impact 3 · Moderate confidence · Owner: Operations lead

Illustrative evidence
E06: the example sharing export contains 12 active external links: five anonymous and seven named-guest links. Two anonymous links point to customer order folders. Example link SHARE-02 has no expiry or recorded owner approval. The sample does not establish whether anyone outside the intended audience accessed it.
Business consequence & rating rationale
A forwarded link could expose customer details or commercial terms. A credible access route supports likelihood 3; the bounded example folder scope supports impact 3, without assuming a reportable breach.
Recommended response
Within seven days, have the data owner review the two customer-folder links and withdraw or replace them with named access as appropriate. By day 30, review all 12 links, require an owner and purpose, and set an expiry or review date. Confirm external collaborators retain only the access needed.
Closure evidence
A before-and-after sharing register; owner approval for retained links; checks that withdrawn links no longer work; named-recipient and review-date records. Document any exception and its expiry.
Delivery & remaining exposure
Operations owns the data decision; the provider assists with settings. Plan one internal working day. Links already used to download copies cannot be recalled by revoking access. Legal or notification decisions would need separate facts and qualified advice.
F06 / Incident coordinationLow · 6 / 25

Technical contacts exist, but business decisions are not rehearsed.

Likelihood 2 · Impact 3 · Limited confidence · Owner: Managing director

Illustrative evidence
E07: a provider contact list is available, but the example material has no agreed incident decision tree, named communications deputy or documented exercise in the quarter. The director expects the provider to advise, but authority to pause payments or dispatch is not recorded.
Business consequence & rating rationale
Confusion can extend disruption or delay containment. Existing reachable contacts limit the assumed chance of a material coordination failure to likelihood 2; additional delay to a core process supports impact 3. If contacts or out-of-hours support cannot be confirmed, revisit this rating.
Recommended response
Assign a decision-maker and deputy, confirm provider availability, and record who can pause payments, isolate a device and approve customer communications. Run a short payment-fraud or unavailable-order-store tabletop during days 61–90. Use a clearly labelled exercise, not a real alert.
Closure evidence
Approved one-page escalation runbook, checked contact details, attendance and decisions from the exercise, and owned follow-up actions. Record unresolved gaps rather than treating attendance as a pass.
Delivery & remaining exposure
Managing director owns decisions; finance, operations and the provider participate. Allow one internal working day across preparation and exercise. A tabletop builds coordination; it does not validate technical recovery or satisfy every contractual or legal obligation.

06 / Illustrative 90-day roadmap

Sequence the work around the business.

Day 1 means management accepts the plan, not the report’s example date. Timing and internal effort are planning assumptions, not a quotation. Provider scope, licences, test capacity and change windows must be agreed separately.

  1. Days 1–7 / Contain avoidable exposure

    Put immediate safeguards in place.

    Finance lead: enforce independent bank-change checks (F01). Office manager: reconcile and remove unneeded leaver access (F03). Operations: review the two customer-folder anonymous links (F05).

    Exit gate: named owners, recorded actions and documented exceptions. Do not delay these actions while evaluating a new tool.

  2. Days 8–30 / Establish a reliable baseline

    Resolve access and device gaps. Agree recovery needs.

    Finance + provider: verify finance MFA enforcement by day 14 and resolve the other four staff exclusions by day 30, or record approved temporary exceptions (F01). IT service owner: reconcile laptops and update exceptions (F04). Operations: complete the 12-link review (F05), nominate all service owners and agree feasible recovery targets with the provider (F02).

    Exit gate: updated registers, checked configuration evidence and an authorised restore-test plan. The provider’s feasibility review is a dependency for the next phase.

  3. Days 31–60 / Demonstrate, do not assume

    Test recovery and sample the new processes.

    Operations + provider: perform isolated recovery exercises for all three critical services and record usable recovery time and data age (F02). Finance: review new bank-change evidence (F01). Office manager: check the leaver process and reconcile accounts again (F03).

    Exit gate: business-owner acceptance of each exercise, gaps against targets, and a funded or explicitly deferred plan for failures. A failed exercise is useful evidence, not a reason to hide the result.

  4. Days 61–90 / Rehearse and reassess

    Turn completed tasks into sustained ownership.

    Managing director: lead the incident tabletop (F06). All owners: present closure evidence, review exceptions and propose ongoing control checks. Re-rate findings only after reviewing what changed.

    Exit gate: an updated risk register marking each item open, in progress, verified closed or explicitly accepted; an owner and review date for every remaining exposure.

Keep a short evidence-led review cadence

Use a weekly owner check-in during the first month, then fortnightly until day 90. Track the actual counts behind each control, overdue actions, recovery-test outcomes and expiring exceptions. Avoid declaring a risk closed because a purchase order was raised or a setting was changed.

Not a recommendation to replace everything

Start by confirming capabilities in existing services and making current processes work. Buy additional capability only where a defined gap remains, an owner can operate it and the business accepts the cost. No precise financial loss or expected return is estimated: this scenario has no validated transaction values, interruption costs or incident-frequency data.

An example closure decision

F03 would remain “in progress” if the four accounts were disabled but no repeatable leaver process was agreed. It could move to “verified closed for the stated finding” after the evidence is reviewed and the new process is demonstrated. That would not mean all identity risk had disappeared.

What a real report would need

Agreed evidence dates and scope, approved collection methods, secure handling of sensitive records, validated owners, management challenge of the findings and an explicit record of unknowns. Conclusions would reflect the evidence actually supplied, not the assumptions in this example.

This is not a compliance certification, penetration-test report or assurance opinion. It does not establish POPIA compliance, prove the absence of compromise or guarantee prevention or recovery. Regulatory and contractual questions need their own scoped review. Ratings and recommendations would change with different evidence, threat exposure, business tolerances or controls.
