Your IT provider is not
your cybersecurity plan.

A common belief, a reasonable assumption, and where it breaks down.

By Johan Taute · Taute Group

When I speak to business owners, they often believe that cybersecurity is their IT provider's responsibility. And that is true, up to a point.

Here is what I see on the other side of that assumption. Providers set a baseline configuration for all of their clients, across the board. The same setup for a law firm and a panel-beating shop, because it keeps things simple to manage. They are difficult to reach in the middle of an incident. And when something does go wrong, the answer often comes back as: we manage the infrastructure, we do not own it. Responsibility, politely moved off their desk and onto yours.

Some providers also skip security best practice, not out of malice, but because good security makes their own jobs harder. Passwords that never expire because resets are a hassle. Password resets done over the phone with no real verification. Small habits that quietly become your attack surface.

What the law actually expects from you

I am not a lawyer, and this is not legal advice. But the direction of the law is worth knowing, because it points at the business owner, not the IT provider.

Under POPIA, your business is the responsible party for the personal information it holds. Section 19 says you must secure the integrity and confidentiality of that information with appropriate, reasonable technical and organisational measures. That includes identifying the risks you face, maintaining safeguards against them, and regularly checking that those safeguards actually work.

Your IT provider is an operator in POPIA's language. Section 21 expects a written contract between you and the operator, under which the operator maintains the security measures. A contract. In writing. With security obligations in it. Many small businesses have never seen such a document for their own IT provider.

And if personal information is compromised, section 22 puts the duty on the responsible party, you, to notify the Information Regulator and the affected people, as soon as reasonably possible. In that moment, the regulator does not phone your IT provider. It phones you.

The Regulator also has real teeth: administrative fines that can run into the millions of rand, and some POPIA offences carry criminal penalties. You do not need to live in fear of that. You do need to be able to show that you took reasonable steps. "My IT guy handles that" is not a step.

So where does a small team start?

For a company of twenty people, cybersecurity should be part of everyone's daily tasks, not a department. Making sure all of you keep the business secure together. When you are not sure about something, ask someone. When something seems strange, speak to someone. Most incidents are noticed by a person before they are noticed by a system.

It is also not an endless hole that swallows you once you start learning. There are ways to prioritise: which tasks matter first, what to look out for, how to keep each other secure.

The first place I would start is with yourself, and one honest question: what steps have I actually implemented to keep my business secure as a baseline?

  • Are laptops left unlocked?
  • Do we have antivirus installed, and is it actually running?
  • Do we have firewalls enabled on the machines and the network?

Not glamorous. But that baseline, written down and actually checked, puts you ahead of a surprising number of businesses your size.
